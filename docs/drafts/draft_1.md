INTRODUCTION
Scientific grants are the essential tools that link new scientific ideas to solutions for grand challenges in health, climate, or energy, the development of new technologies, or the pursuit of basic research projects that expand the knowledge space.  The grant proposals are generated in a decentralized system where individual scientists put forth ideas based on their training, experiences, and interests and from that pool expert peer reviewers by  who determine which ideas are novel, promising, risky, feasible, and thus worth funding. Grant review  involves two linked stages of collective search: the generation of candidate scientific futures and the filtering of those futures through peer review.  

Artificial intelligence is now entering both stages with a powerful promise to scale scientific research. AI systems can automatically generate research ideas, search literature, draft proposals, and carry out experiments [CITE]. They can also automatically summarize proposals, identify strengths and weaknesses, and generate evaluative judgments. In some domains, this promise is already credible. AlphaFold, for example, demonstrated that AI could scale and transform a long-standing scientific bottleneck in protein structure prediction [CITE]. 

Yet this promised efficiency may carry a collective cost as the reliance on AI may narrow the diversity of ideas being explored and judgements being applied to those ideas. Recent work on generative AI and creativity shows that AI assistance can improve individual outputs while reducing collective diversity (Doshi and Hauser, 2024). Related science-of-science work suggests that AI adoption may expand individual scientific impact while concentrating focus in more established, data-rich areas of science (Hao et al., 2024). This cost is especially important for grantmaking because science advances depend on the diversity in both generation and selection. Diversity in ideas is more likely to encounter unexpected discoveries and breakthroughs, where diverse judgements are more likely to uncover different weaknesses and paths for improvement.  AI could compress scientific search twice: first by producing a narrower distribution of candidate ideas, and second by applying more homogeneous evaluative filters to those ideas.

This collective cost has been difficult to study empirically as it requires data that is rarely available. Grant proposals and expert reviews are often confidential. While results from where participants generate ideas and reviews provide valuable signals (Doshi and Hauser, 2024), those settings don’t carry the same realistic stake as in grant funding. Most evaluations of AI for science also often focus on the quality, usefulness, and correctness of individual proposals or reviews [CITE]. Such studies are valuable, but do not measure and compare AI’s impact on the collective level of scientific ideas and filtering. If we were to use publicly available grant proposals and reviews and compare them against those by AI, it is likely that the AI models have already been trained on those data. 

Here we study whether frontier AI models broaden or compress scientific search and filtering in the context of biomedical research proposals and reviews, using a unique dataset of 23 grant proposals submitted to a foundation that gives out $xx of funding each year to support groundbreaking research in molecular and cellular biosciences, and N reviews for these proposals written by panels of senior scientists. We set up an experiment where the frontier AI models draft proposals given the same call for proposals that human scientists received, and write reviews given the same criteria given to human reviewers. We then compare the proposals and reviews by human scientists and AI models to examine two questions: (1) Do AI models generate proposal sets that are as diverse and novel as those produced by human scientists? (2) Do AI systems provide feedback as diverse as those produced by expert human reviewers?

Using embedding model trained on biomedical text, we quantify proposal diversity, novelty, and review diversity. We find three main results. First, human-authored proposals occupy a broader semantic search space than AI-authored proposals across multiple measures of dispersion, sparseness, local isolation, and coverage of topic regions. Second, human proposals are more novel relative to a biomedical literature corpus from PubMed, whereas AI proposals tend to sit closer to dense regions of existing research. Third, human expert reviews are more semantically diverse than AI reviews, and AI reviews are more similar to each other than human reviews are. Together, these findings indicate that AI may compress scientific search at both stages: by narrowing the generation of candidate ideas and by narrowing the evaluative filters through which those ideas are judged.

Our contribution is to shift the evaluation of AI for science from individual output quality to collective search. The central question is not only whether an AI system can produce a plausible proposal or a reasonable review. It is whether the use of AI changes the distribution of ideas being generated and the distribution of judgments being applied to those ideas. Scientific institutions should therefore evaluate AI not only as a tool for efficiency, but also as a force that may reshape the diversity of scientific imagination and evaluation.


DATA
We constructed a balanced dataset of human- and AI-authored biomedical research proposals and matched human and AI reviews. The human proposal set consists of 23 research proposals written by faculty-level scientists in response to the same biomedical funding opportunity. Twelve proposals came from the first year of the program and eleven from the second year. These proposals were written by expert scientists in biomedical domains and represent candidate research directions submitted to a real funding process.

To construct a comparable AI proposal set, we gave the same call for proposals to three frontier AI models: Claude, Gemini, and GPT. Each model was asked to generate 23 distinct proposal ideas and then write a full proposal for each idea. The AI models were prompted with the same funding call so that human and AI proposals were aligned to the same task environment. This produced 69 AI-authored proposals, yielding 92 proposals in total.

We also analyzed reviews of the 23 human-authored proposals. Each human proposal received three to five independent expert reviews, for a total of 85 human reviews. Reviewers were senior scientific experts and used the same evaluation criteria. For the AI review condition, each of the three AI models reviewed each human proposal using the same criteria and descriptions provided to human reviewers, producing 69 AI reviews. This matched design allows comparison between human and AI evaluations of the same underlying proposals.

To evaluate literature-relative novelty and literature-region coverage, we constructed a biomedical literature corpus of 39,538 PubMed abstracts published between January 1, 2010 and May 25, 2026. This corpus serves as the reference space for measuring how far each proposal sits from existing scientific work and which regions of the biomedical literature each proposal engages.

All proposals and reviews were first rephrased into a standardized format using Gemini to reduce style-level confounding. This step was designed to ensure that embedding-based comparisons captured semantic content rather than surface differences in prose style, formatting, or rhetorical polish. We then embedded the standardized texts using BioLinkBERT-large, a transformer encoder model pretrained on biomedical text.

VARIABLES
We measure four outcomes: proposal diversity, topic-region coverage, proposal novelty, and review diversity.
First, we operationalize proposal diversity as the breadth, dispersion, and local isolation of proposals in semantic embedding space. Pairwise distance captures the average separation among proposals within the same author group. Centroid dispersion measures how far proposals sit from their group’s or the global semantic center. Minimum-spanning-tree dispersion captures how broadly a group covers a connected region of semantic space. Sparseness measures whether proposals cluster around a representative medoid or spread away from it. Nearest-neighbor isolation measures whether proposals have close local neighbors, indicating potential redundancy. Together, these metrics capture complementary forms of collective idea diversity rather than relying on a single distance measure.
Second, we measure topic coverage to make the semantic-diversity results more interpretable. We first use BERTopic to create different topic regions on the fixed embedding map of the biomedical literature, then project proposals into this map and identify which topic regions each proposal’s nearest literature neighbors occupy. We also extract the MeSH terms of those neighbors to compare the coverage of unique MeSH terms between author groups. A proposal set that covers more literature regions and more unique MeSH terms reflects broader domain coverage, whereas a proposal set concentrated in one or two regions suggests narrower topical search.

Third, we operationalize novelty as the distance between proposal embeddings and the biomedical literature corpus. MeanKNN novelty measures how far each proposal is from its nearest existing literature neighbors, using k values of 5, 10, 20, and 50. ElementNovel measures how unusual a proposal is relative to the local structure of the literature space. These measures do not imply that a proposal is better, more feasible, or more scientifically correct. They indicate whether a proposal is more remote from, or more anchored in, the existing literature baseline.

Fourth, we operationalize review diversity as the semantic spread of independent reviews written for the same proposal. Review diversity captures whether different reviewers bring distinct evaluative lenses to the same scientific idea. In grantmaking, reviews do more than assign scores. They identify risks, interpret originality, assess feasibility, surface missing evidence, and suggest paths for improvement. A review process with diverse feedback can therefore reveal more dimensions of an uncertain proposal than a review process in which all reviewers converge on the same critique. We compare human and AI reviews using the same family of embedding-based diversity metrics used for proposals. 

RESULTS
Result 1: Human proposals occupy a broader semantic search space than AI proposals




Proposals by human scientists occupied a broader semantic search space than those by AI models. Across different metrics for diversity including pairwise distance, centroid dispersion (within group and global), and medoid sparseness, human proposals were more dispersed and diverse than AI proposals. They showed greater average separation among proposals, weaker clustering around their own group and global centroid, and broader coverage of semantic space.
The clearest difference appears in the pairwise-distance distribution. Human proposals had a mean within-group pairwise distance of 0.4429, compared with 0.0337 for Claude, 0.1505 for Gemini, and 0.3148 for GPT-5.2. All three model contrasts were significant after Holm correction: Claude versus Human, Δ = −0.3670, Cliff’s δ = −1.000, p < .001; Gemini versus Human, Δ = −0.2838, δ = −0.826, p < .001; GPT-5.2 versus Human, Δ = −0.1546, δ = −0.478, p < .01. The same ordering was visible in centroid-dispersion (local and global), MST-dispersion, and medoid sparseness. 

Result 2: Human proposals cover broader regions and topics within the biomedical literature map



Fig. 2 places proposals within a fixed map of the biomedical literature. The background map is fitted on 39,538 PubMed abstracts, and literature regions are defined using BERT-topic regions. Proposal embeddings are then projected into this fixed landscape. This visualization complements the proposal-only embedding analysis by showing where human and AI proposals fall relative to the broader biomedical literature corpus.
The region-coverage panel shows that human proposals covered a broader set of literature regions than several AI models. Human proposals had a region breadth of 3, Shannon entropy of 1.1583, effective region count of 3.1845, and dominant-region fraction of 0.5217. Claude proposals were almost entirely concentrated in a single region, with breadth = 1, entropy = 0.0155, effective region count = 1.0156, and dominant-region fraction = 0.9978. Gemini proposals also showed higher concentration, with breadth = 2, entropy = 0.3790, effective region count = 1.4608, and dominant-region fraction = 0.9130. GPT-5.2 was closer to Human, with breadth = 3, entropy = 0.8587, effective region count = 2.3600, and dominant-region fraction = 0.7370.
A complementary MeSH analysis provided a curated biomedical vocabulary check. Human proposals had mean unique MeSH coverage of 79.57 and a group-level union of 750 unique MeSH terms. Claude proposals had mean unique MeSH coverage of 62.39 and a union of 197 terms. Gemini proposals had mean unique MeSH coverage of 57.52 and a union of 339 terms. GPT-5.2 proposals had mean unique MeSH coverage of 68.30 and a union of 450 terms.

Result 4: Human reviewers provide more diverse evaluative filters than AI reviewers

Fig. 4 compares the semantic diversity of human and AI reviews written for the same human-authored proposals. In Y1, the matched review set included 47 human reviews and 36 AI reviews across 12 proposals. In Y2, it included 38 human reviews and 33 AI reviews across 11 proposals. The analysis compares review diversity within each proposal, so the human and AI reviews are evaluated against the same proposal-level targets.

In Y1, four of nine whole-review diversity metrics were significant after Wilcoxon FDR correction: span90, global-centroid distance, medoid distance, and sparseness, each with q = 0.047241.
