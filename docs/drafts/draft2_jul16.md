## INTRODUCTION

Scientific grants are the essential link between new scientific ideas and solutions to grand
challenges in health, climate, and energy — funding the development of new technologies and the
basic research that expands what is known. Proposals arise in a decentralized system: individual
scientists put forward ideas shaped by their training, experience, and interests, and from that
pool expert peer reviewers determine which ideas are novel, promising, risky, and feasible enough
to fund. Grant review is therefore two linked stages of collective search — the *generation* of
candidate scientific futures, and the *filtering* of those futures through peer review. The health
of science depends not only on the quality of any single proposal or review, but on the diversity
of what is generated and the diversity of the judgments applied to it.

Artificial intelligence is now entering both stages, with a powerful promise to scale scientific
work. AI systems can generate research ideas, search the literature, draft proposals, and even
execute experiments [CITE]. They can also summarize proposals, surface strengths and weaknesses,
and produce evaluative judgments [CITE]. In some domains this promise is already credible:
AlphaFold showed that AI could transform a long-standing bottleneck in protein-structure
prediction [CITE]. As these systems improve, it is increasingly plausible that AI could be used
not merely to assist individual scientists but to automate generation and review at scale.

Yet this efficiency may carry a collective cost. If scientists and institutions come to rely on a
small number of frontier models, the diversity of ideas explored and of judgments applied to them
may narrow — even as the quality of any individual output rises. Recent work on generative AI and
creativity finds exactly this tension: AI assistance can improve individual outputs while reducing
the collective diversity of what a population produces (Doshi and Hauser, 2024). Related
science-of-science work suggests AI adoption may expand individual scientific impact while
concentrating attention on more established, data-rich areas (Hao et al., 2024). We call the
resulting risk **double compression**: AI may narrow scientific search twice — first by generating
a narrower distribution of candidate ideas, and second by applying more homogeneous evaluative
filters to whatever ideas survive. Diversity matters at both stages for distinct reasons. Diverse
generation raises the chance that an unexpected, high-value direction is proposed at all; diverse
filtering raises the chance that a proposal's hidden weaknesses, or its overlooked promise, are
seen by *someone* on the panel. Compression at either stage narrows the funnel; compression at
both compounds.

This collective cost has been hard to study, for three reasons. First, grant proposals and expert
reviews are typically confidential, so the raw material of scientific search is rarely available
for analysis. Second, controlled ideation studies, while valuable (Doshi and Hauser, 2024), do not
carry the stakes, expertise, or institutional realism of actual grant funding. Third, most
evaluations of AI for science measure the *quality*, usefulness, or correctness of individual
proposals or reviews [CITE] — an individual-output lens that cannot, by construction, detect a
change in the *distribution* of ideas or judgments. And the most obvious empirical shortcut —
comparing public proposals and reviews against AI-generated ones — is confounded, because frontier
models have likely trained on any publicly available proposals.

Here we study whether frontier AI models broaden or compress scientific search and filtering in
biomedical research, using a dataset that avoids these problems: 23 grant proposals submitted to a
foundation that awards [NUM: $xx] annually for groundbreaking research in molecular and cellular
biosciences, together with [NUM: N] expert reviews of those proposals written by panels of senior
scientists. Because these proposals and reviews come from a real, confidential funding process,
they are unlikely to appear in any model's training data. We construct a matched experiment in
which three frontier models (Claude, Gemini, and GPT) draft proposals from the *same* call for
proposals given to the human scientists, and write reviews under the *same* criteria given to the
human reviewers. We then compare human and AI outputs in a shared semantic space to ask two
questions: (1) do AI models generate proposal sets as diverse as those produced by human
scientists? and (2) do AI systems provide feedback as diverse as that of expert human reviewers?

Critically, we do not evaluate AI under a single generation strategy. A natural objection to any
finding of AI homogeneity is that it reflects naive prompting rather than a property of the models
— and prior work has shown that AI idea diversity, while consistently below human levels, is
sensitive to how models are prompted (Meincke et al., 2024; De Freitas et al., 2025). Idea
generation is a bottleneck: downstream screening and refinement can improve the quality of existing
candidates but cannot restore diversity that was never generated (Meincke et al., 2024). We
therefore stress-test AI generation and review against the diversity interventions the literature
has identified, using **three conditions** of increasing diversity pressure:

- A *baseline* condition, in which each model produces the full set of 23 ideas (or all reviews for
  a proposal) in a single pass. This is the common, low-effort way to query a model for a set, and
  it is precisely the setup shown to yield high pairwise similarity among AI ideas (Meincke et al.,
  2024).
- A *one-at-a-time* condition, in which each idea or review is drawn from an independent model call
  returning a single item. This removes within-context homogenization — the tendency of a model to
  condition each new item on the ones it has already produced and collapse toward its own mode — and
  mirrors the structure of a human population, where each scientist generates independently.
- A *persona* condition, in which each independent call is additionally conditioned on a distinct
  persona card matched to the profile of a real human scientist in the applicant pool. Persona and
  perspective conditioning is the most widely used technique for eliciting diverse outputs from LLMs
  and for simulating population heterogeneity (Meincke et al., 2024; De Freitas et al., 2025; [CITE
  multi-persona ideation, Design Science 2025]), making it the strongest readily available
  intervention against homogeneity.

This design lets us distinguish a property of the models from an artifact of prompting: if AI
narrowing were merely lazy prompting, it should ease under one-at-a-time and dissolve under persona.
Recent evidence makes this a live question rather than a foregone conclusion — persona conditioning
can *fail* to deliver the diversity it promises, with richer persona specifications sometimes
contracting rather than expanding representational diversity ("persona manifold collapse"; [CITE
persona-collapse 2026]; [CITE Kambhatla et al., 2025]). Our persona condition therefore tests not
only our own hypothesis but a broader claim in the field about whether persona prompting is an
adequate remedy for AI homogeneity at all. [CHECK: state the persona result plainly in the final
results sentence — this is the paper's central bet.]

We report two linked findings that together define double compression. First, in **generation**,
human-authored proposals occupy a broader semantic search space than AI-authored proposals across
every condition: they contain more effectively distinct ideas, vary along more independent
conceptual dimensions, and cover more of both the human search region and the biomedical literature
landscape, while AI proposals concentrate into denser, more repetitive clusters. Second, in
**filtering**, human expert review panels are more semantically diverse than AI review panels
evaluating the *same* proposal: given an identical proposal, AI reviews converge on a narrower set of
critiques than human reviewers do. Most importantly, these gaps **persist under the persona
condition** — narrowing survives even when we explicitly engineer AI generation and review for
diversity using the field's strongest available intervention — and they follow a consistent ordering
across models [CHECK: report the Claude/Gemini/GPT gradient direction]. Together these results
indicate that AI can compress scientific search at both stages, and that the compression is a robust
property of current frontier models rather than an artifact of how they are prompted.

Our contribution is to move the evaluation of AI for science from individual output quality to
collective search. The question is not only whether an AI system can produce a plausible proposal
or a reasonable review; it is whether adopting AI changes the distribution of ideas generated and
of judgments applied to them. Scientific institutions should evaluate AI not only as a tool for
efficiency, but as a force that may reshape the diversity of scientific imagination and evaluation
— and our conditions show that mitigating this narrowing is harder than better prompting alone.

## VARIABLES / MEASURES

Our central construct is the *narrowing* of collective search: whether AI-generated proposals and
reviews occupy a smaller, more redundant region of semantic space than human ones. Narrowing is not
a single quantity. A set of ideas can be tightly packed on average, yet also — separately —
repetitive, low-dimensional, or confined to a corner of the space others explore. These are
distinct ways of being "less diverse," and a single distance number cannot tell them apart. We
therefore decompose diversity into five complementary facets, each answering a different question
about how a set of ideas fills the search space, and we treat them as related-but-non-redundant
angles on narrowing rather than as independent findings (Hill, 1973; Jost, 2006; Chao et al., 2014).
We then add a sixth, directional measure — *displacement* — which asks not how much space a set
fills but whether it sits in a *different* region than the human set. All measures are computed in
the full BioLinkBERT embedding space on L2-normalized vectors, separately for each author group,
and, where group sizes differ, at matched sample size.

**Facet 1 — Spread / dispersion (how far apart ideas are).** The most direct sense of diversity is
how far ideas sit from one another and from their center. We measure mean pairwise distance (the
average separation among proposals in a set), centroid dispersion (how far proposals sit from their
group's semantic center), minimum-spanning-tree dispersion (how broadly a set covers a connected
region), and sparseness (whether proposals cluster around a representative medoid or spread away from
it). These capture the intuition at the heart of the hypothesis — that AI ideas and reviews grow
*closer and more similar to one another*. Because they are largely alternative views of the same
underlying scale, we report mean pairwise distance as the primary spread measure and the others as
convergent support, not as separate results. [This is the corrected framing: spread is one facet,
reported through several convergent metrics — not six independent findings.]

**Facet 2 — Evenness (uniform spread vs. clumping and repetition).** At a fixed overall spread, a set
can be evenly distributed or piled into a few dense, repeated clusters. Evenness distinguishes these.
We capture it two ways. First, through the slope of the diversity profile (Facet 3), which falls
steeply when a few dominant modes carry most of the mass. Second, and more directly, through local
repetition: we measure nearest-neighbor distance distributions and a Ripley-style neighbor-count
function in the full embedding space, evaluated against a resampling null drawn from the pooled cloud
(Ripley, 1977; Myllymäki et al., 2017). Excess mass at short range indicates near-duplication —
proposals or reviews with very close neighbors. This is the facet that formalizes the dense, repeated
clusters visible when AI proposals are projected into two dimensions, without relying on the
projection itself.

**Facet 3 — Richness (effective number of distinct ideas).** Beyond how far apart ideas are, we ask
how many genuinely *distinct* ideas a set is worth once near-duplicates are discounted. We compute
the Vendi Score — the exponential of the entropy of the eigenvalues of the set's similarity kernel
(Friedman and Dieng, 2023; Pasarkar and Dieng, 2024), equivalent to a similarity-sensitive Hill
number (Leinster and Cobbold, 2012). Reported across a range of orders *q*, it traces a diversity
profile whose low-*q* end reflects richness (how many distinct modes appear at all) and whose slope
toward higher *q* reflects evenness (Facet 2). A set of near-duplicates collapses to a low effective
number even when its average pairwise distance is moderate — capturing repetition that spread metrics
average away. This is our primary diversity measure.

**Facet 4 — Dimensionality (how many independent axes ideas vary along).** A set can string many
distinct ideas along a single conceptual axis, or scatter a few across many. We measure the number of
independent axes of variation using the participation ratio of the set's embedding covariance
spectrum (Del Giudice, 2021). A low participation ratio means variation is essentially one- or
two-dimensional. Reporting dimensionality alongside richness tells us *which kind* of narrowing AI
exhibits — fewer distinct ideas, fewer axes of variation, or both.

**Facet 5 — Coverage (how much of the search space is occupied, and whose).** Our hypothesis is
directional: AI narrows *relative to humans*. We therefore measure how much of the space humans
explore is reached by AI, in two complementary ways. Geometrically, we compute support-overlap
coverage (Naeem et al., 2020): the fraction of the human proposal region that falls within reach of
AI proposals, whose complement is the fraction of the human search space AI never proposes. We
benchmark this against a human self-coverage baseline — coverage of one random half of the human set
by the other — to establish what coverage looks like when both sets are genuinely human at this
sample size. (We avoid convex-hull volume as a coverage proxy, since in high-dimensional embedding
space hull volume is dominated by a few outliers and is not a reliable measure of occupied region.)
Domain-grounded, we also measure literature coverage: using BERTopic, we define topic regions on the
fixed PubMed embedding landscape, project each proposal onto it, and record which regions each
proposal's nearest literature neighbors occupy, along with the unique MeSH terms of those neighbors.
A set spanning more literature regions and more unique MeSH terms covers more of the biomedical
landscape; one concentrated in a few regions searches more narrowly. Geometric and literature
coverage together show both *that* AI covers less space and *which* areas of biomedical science it
under-explores.

**Directional check — Displacement (same region, or shifted?).** The five facets above all measure
the *size and shape* of a set's occupied region. A distinct question is whether AI's region sits in a
*different* place than the human region — narrowed toward a shared central zone, or shifted into its
own subregion. We quantify displacement between the human and AI point clouds using a distributional
distance (maximum mean discrepancy and/or an optimal-transport distance) [CITE Gretton et al., 2012].
We keep displacement conceptually separate from the diversity facets because it answers "same place?"
not "how much space?": a set could be displaced without being narrower, or narrower without being
displaced. Reporting it lets us state not only that AI narrows, but whether it narrows *toward a
common region* — a stronger and more specific claim about homogenization. [This replaces "displacement
as a diversity facet"; it is a separate directional construct.]

**Review diversity (paired, within-proposal).** Because each proposal is reviewed by a panel, review
diversity must be measured *within* a proposal to separate reviewer disagreement from differences
between proposals. For each human proposal, we compute the semantic spread of the reviews it received
— using the same facets above — for the human panel and for each matched AI panel of equal size drawn
from that model's review reservoir for that proposal. Reviews do more than assign scores: they
identify risks, interpret originality, assess feasibility, surface missing evidence, and suggest
improvements. A panel whose reviews are semantically diverse surfaces more dimensions of an uncertain
proposal than one whose reviews converge on the same critique. We compare human and AI review
diversity as a paired contrast across the 23 proposals — each proposal contributing one human value
and one AI value — which holds the reviewed object fixed.

**Estimation and inference.** Diversity measures are sample-size dependent, so all cross-group
comparisons are made at matched sample size — directly for per-model comparisons (23 vs. 23) and by
subsampling for pooled human-vs-all-AI comparisons. For unpaired proposal comparisons, significance
is assessed by label-permutation tests; for paired within-proposal review comparisons, by Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests; and the model ordering (Claude, Gemini, GPT, human) is tested as an ordered trend
(Jonckheere–Terpstra). We report effect sizes with confidence intervals throughout, correct for
multiple comparisons across the metric family, and confirm that conclusions hold on both the
style-standardized ("rephrased") text — the primary analysis — and the original text as a robustness
check. Full definitions, estimators, and null constructions are given in Materials and Methods / SI.
