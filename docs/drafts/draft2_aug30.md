Double compression: AI narrows both the generation and the gatekeeping of scientific ideas
ABSTRACT
Scientific grant review has two stages that both depend on diversity: many investigators propose ideas, and panels of reviewers judge which ideas to fund. Large language models can now perform both tasks, yet evaluations of them measure the quality of individual proposals and reviews and leave unmeasured whether AI narrows the range of ideas proposed and the judgments applied to them, a question that has been hard to study because realistic data is rarely available. Here we compare 23 proposals submitted to a real molecular and cellular bioscience competition, together with their expert reviews, against proposals and reviews produced by Claude, Gemini, and GPT from the same call and the same criteria, under three prompting conditions of increasing diversity pressure. AI compresses diversity at both stages. In generation, AI proposal sets hold 25 to 36% fewer distinct ideas than human sets and repeat near-duplicates, even as they engage the same research topics and occupy the same region of idea space, reproducing its dense core while under-representing the distinctive ideas at its edges. In gate-keeping, AI reviewers of a given proposal converge on one another and on nearly identical scores, and AI panel scores show no detectable association with the competition’s funding decisions. Persona conditioning, the strongest documented intervention against model homogeneity, removes the difference in wording but not the difference in ideas or judgments.

Significance statement: Science depends on diversity at two stages: many researchers propose different ideas, and many reviewers disagree about which are worth funding. AI systems can now do both jobs, and the case for using them rests on how good each proposal or review is on its own. Using 23 confidential proposals to a real biomedical competition and their reviews by a panel of senior scientists, we compared the diversity of what scientists produced with what three leading AI systems produced from the same instructions. AI proposed a quarter to a third fewer distinct ideas, and AI reviewers agreed with one another so closely that their scores could not identify which proposals were funded. Asking the models to be more varied changed their wording, not their ideas.

INTRODUCTION
Scientific grants are the primary mechanism that links new ideas to the resources needed to pursue them, whether basic research that expands knowledge or technologies and solutions for challenges in health, climate, and energy. Grant review is a two-stage process of collective search: investigators generate candidate ideas, and peer reviewers filter them. Proposals come from a decentralized pool of scientists shaped by their training and interests, and reviewers judge which ideas are novel, feasible, and worth funding.

Large language models are now entering both stages, with the promise of scaling science and accelerating discovery by automating ideation, execution, and review [CITE]. On the generation side, they can produce research ideas, search the literature, draft proposals, and run experiments [CITE]. On the review side, they can summarize proposals, identify strengths and weaknesses, and issue evaluative judgments. In some domains the promise is already credible: AlphaFold transformed a long-standing bottleneck in protein structure prediction [CITE], and recent venues have published research articles written and reviewed entirely by AI agents [CITE].

Yet this efficiency may carry a collective cost, because reliance on AI could narrow the diversity of ideas explored and of judgments applied to them. Recent work on generative AI and creativity finds that AI assistance can improve individual outputs while reducing collective diversity (Doshi and Hauser, 2024), and science-of-science work suggests that AI adoption can expand individual scientific impact while concentrating attention on more established, data-rich areas (Hao et al., 2024). The cost matters especially for grantmaking, because scientific progress depends on diversity at both stages: diverse generation is more likely to surface unexpected discoveries, and diverse filtering is more likely to catch hidden weaknesses or overlooked strengths. AI could therefore compress scientific search twice, a problem we call double compression: first by generating a narrower set of candidates, then by filtering them with more uniform judgment.

Double compression has been difficult to study empirically, because it requires data that is rarely available. Grant proposals and expert reviews are usually confidential. Laboratory studies in which participants, often graduate students, generate and review ideas offer valuable insight (e.g., Doshi and Hauser, 2024; Si et al., 2024), but they do not carry the stakes of a real funding competition or the expertise of the senior scientists who write and review grant proposals. Public data carries a different problem: models were likely trained on it. Most evaluations of AI for science focus on the quality, usefulness, or correctness of individual proposals or reviews [CITE], which is valuable but does not measure AI’s effect on diversity at the collective level of generation and filtering.

We study double compression with data that carries the stakes and expertise of real grant funding, using a design that separates a property of the models from the artifacts of prompting and writing style. Our corpus is the 23 proposals submitted to a real molecular and cellular bioscience competition, the NSF National Synthesis Center for Emergence in the Molecular and Cellular Sciences (NCEMS), together with the expert reviews each proposal received from senior scientists. Because the competition is confidential, neither the proposals nor the reviews are likely to appear in any model's training data.

Three frontier models, Claude, Gemini, and GPT, drafted proposals from the same call given to the human applicants and reviewed the same proposals under the same criteria given to the human reviewers, which holds the task environment fixed across humans and AI. We prompted the models under three conditions of rising diversity pressure. In baseline, a single request returns the whole set. In one-at-a-time, each item comes from an independent call, which removes the within-context homogenization by which a model conditions each new item on the ones before it. In persona, each independent call is conditioned on a distinct scientist persona matched to a real applicant. Persona conditioning is the strongest documented intervention against language-model homogeneity (Meincke et al. 2024; De Freitas et al. 2025), so if the narrowing were a prompting artifact, it should ease under one-at-a-time and dissolve under persona. We ask two questions: whether AI models generate proposal sets as diverse as those of human scientists, and whether AI panels review a given proposal as diversely as human panels.

AI reduces diversity at both stages, the two effects differ, and prompting for more diversity removes neither. In generation, AI proposes fewer distinct ideas, drawn from the same topics and the same region of idea space as human proposals rather than a separate one, and it reproduces the dense core of human ideas while under-representing their distinctive edges. In review, each AI review sits close to the human reviews, yet the AI reviews of a proposal resemble one another and their scores barely differ, whereas human reviews of the same proposal diverge. Prompting for more diversity changes the wording but not the ideas or judgments. We therefore argue that AI for science should be evaluated by its effect on the range of ideas and judgments across proposals and reviews, not only by the quality of individual ones, and we show that better prompting does not remove the effect.

RESULTS

Twenty-three proposals submitted to a real biomedical funding competition (two applicant cohorts), and the 85 expert reviews they received (2 to 5 per proposal), are compared with proposals and reviews produced by three frontier models from the same call and the same review criteria (five AI reviews per model per proposal, 345 in all), under three prompting conditions of increasing diversity pressure. Generation compares whole sets at matched size (23 against 23); gate-keeping compares each proposal's AI review panel with the human panel of that same proposal at matched panel size. We compared human and AI outputs in a shared biomedical embedding space (Fig. 1). Because wording and stylistic differences can make two texts appear different when their content is the same [CITE], every proposal and review by both human and AI was rewritten into one standardized format and style through Gemini before embedding. The original text was retained as a robustness check (Materials and Methods). We measured diversity through four facets: (1) spread, how far apart the ideas sit from one another on average, measured by mean pairwise distance [CITE]; (2) richness, the number of effectively distinct ideas in a set, measured by the Vendi score [CITE] and the Simpson index [CITE]; (3) evenness, whether ideas are evenly spaced or piled into near-duplicate clumps; and (4) coverage, how much of the human idea territory each set occupies and how evenly, measured both geometrically in the embedding space and by where proposals land in an embedding of the existing literature, together with a displacement check for whether the two sets occupy the same region. All comparisons are made at matched sample size. Generation effects on proposals are group ratios where 1.0 is human parity, comparing the human set with pooled AI: the three models' 69 proposals subsampled to 23 to match the human set, averaged over 1,000 draws. Gate-keeping effects are measured on reviews paired within proposal and reported as Cliff's δ, where δ = −1 means the AI review panel was less diverse for every one of the proposals.

Spread and richness. Proposals in AI sets are less spread out and less rich than those in the human sets (Fig. 2A). Human proposals average 0.416 apart in mean pairwise distance, while pooled AI proposals average 0.268 to 0.293, or 64% to 70% of human spread, a gap that is significant at baseline (p = 0.010), one-at-a-time (p < 0.001), and persona (p = 0.003). The effective number of distinct ideas, measured by the Vendi score, follows the same pattern: each condition retains 66% to 75% of human richness (p = 0.005, < 0.001, and 0.004, respectively). The Simpson index, a classic measure of ecological diversity, reproduces this result (SI Appendix, SI-5).
Evenness. AI proposals also cluster into near-duplicates. In every condition, human proposals are more evenly spaced than a random draw of the same size (close-neighbor excess −0.141, −0.234, −0.115), whereas AI proposals are more crowded than chance (+0.077, +0.142, +0.062; P = 0.001; Fig. 2B); negative values mean more regular spacing than chance and positive values mean clumping. The contrast is sharpest pair by pair: at the separation where about 35% of human proposals have a near-duplicate, more than 80% of Claude's baseline proposals do. In a grant competition, applicants have every reason to differentiate their ideas from one another, yet a model asked for 23 proposals in a single pass has none.

Same topics, occupied less evenly. AI proposals sit in the same topical regions as human proposals. We embedded a corpus of PubMed papers retrieved with search terms from the call, grouped them into 12 topic regions with BERTopic, and compared the regions and MeSH terms of each proposal's nearest neighbors on this map. All sets touch 5 to 8 of the 12 regions and engage similar numbers of unique MeSH terms (571 to 598 for AI sets against 504 for human sets, not significant); only one cell survives correction, pooled AI under one-at-a-time at 5.9 regions against 7 (P<sub>FDR</sub> = 0.008). The generation narrowing (Result A) is therefore within topics, not avoidance of them. Occupancy of those topics is uneven, however. Treating each region as a category, the inverse-Simpson effective number of regions occupied is 4.94 for human proposals against 3.1 to 3.9 for pooled AI at matched sample size (ratios 0.78, 0.62, 0.73), so AI spreads across fewer regions in every condition, most severely under one-at-a-time (Fig. 3A).
Geometry. The two sets are not separated. A two-sample distance test cannot distinguish them in any condition (MMD² = 0.037, 0.062, 0.032, all not significant), benchmarked against a calibration in which two halves of the human sample differ by approximately 0, and an independent optimal-transport distance agrees. Gemini is the one partial exception, significantly displaced under one-at-a-time and persona, whereas GPT is essentially undisplaced (persona MMD² = 0.000). The AI cloud is thus concentric with the human cloud and shares its radial extent; what differs is how evenly it fills that space (Result A).
The missing ideas are the distinctive ones. The proposals an equal-sized AI pool fails to reproduce are not a random fifth; they are the most distinctive. How far a human proposal sits from the center of human idea space predicts, almost deterministically, how often a matched AI pool has no proposal near it (Spearman ρ = +0.86 between distinctiveness and miss-rate, P < 0.001; ρ = −0.99 for local density; Fig. 3B). This is not an artifact of how the quantities are defined: distinctiveness is computed from human-to-human distances alone, and miss-rate from human-to-AI distances. Split by density, AI misses none of the dense core, 4% of the middle, and 69% of the distinctive edge, and the pattern replicates on raw text (0%, 13%, 58%). AI reproduces the dense core of human scientific ideas and under-represents their sparse edges.

This lost territory is one-sided, and it is not the weaker ideas that go missing. An equal-sized AI pool reaches only 81% to 86% of the human idea region, below the human self-benchmark (P = 0.038 at baseline, P < 0.001 at persona, marginal at one-at-a-time). The uncovered part is almost entirely human: 18% to 26% of human proposals have no AI proposal within normal human spacing, about twice the ~10% that humans leave among themselves by construction, and widest under persona (26%); only 4% to 9% of AI proposals have no human counterpart, at or below that reference (Fig. 3B, inset). AI thus proposes almost no idea that humans do not also reach, yet leaves roughly one human proposal in five unmatched. Those missed proposals are statistically indistinguishable from the reproduced ones in the competition's funding and ranking (funded versus rejected miss-rate 0.20 versus 0.26, Mann-Whitney P = 0.77; all P > 0.3), so the gap is not AI skipping the ideas that human reviewers themselves rejected. Generation therefore loses territory specifically at its distinctive edges. Whether gate-keeping loses anything comparable is the second question.

Low spread and richness, but high coverage. Given the same proposal, the reviews in the AI panel resemble one another more than those in the human panel do, in the same direction for nearly every proposal (Fig. 4A). Under one-at-a-time, pooled δ = −0.91 for spread and −0.83 for richness, with several model-level cells at δ = −1.00, meaning the AI panel is narrower for every one of the 23 proposals, the floor the exact paired test can express at this sample size; persona is similar (δ = −0.74). At baseline the pattern holds for Claude and GPT (δ = −0.74 to −0.91), but Gemini sits at parity (δ = +0.04, not significant), which dilutes the pooled contrast to marginal (δ ≈ −0.3 to −0.4, P = 0.07 to 0.09). AI panels also carry excess near-duplicate mass relative to same-size random panels (δ = +0.91 at one-at-a-time, +0.74 at persona). At the same time, pooled AI panels cover 96% to 97% of the human review span (89% to 98% per model), exceeding the human self-benchmark of 83%, the share of each human review covered by the rest of its own panel (δ = +0.37 to +0.44 in every condition). Combined with the smaller spread, this is the signature of redundancy, not completeness: each AI review individually resembles the human reviews, but the panel as a whole adds no new perspective. It behaves like a central blanket, several copies of one competent, centrally positioned reviewer, whereas a human panel is a set of distinct voices that disagree informatively. What gate-keeping loses is not the quality of any single judgment but the independence of judgments across the panel.

Compression is concentrated in the criticism. The effect is strongest when we split each review into its strengths and its weaknesses and compare them separately (Fig. 4B). Weakness panels compress at the design ceiling (δ = −0.91 for both spread and richness, narrower for 22 of 23 proposals), while strengths compress substantially less (δ = −0.57 to −0.65). Weaknesses are also where human panels have the most diversity to lose: human within-panel spread is 0.084 for weaknesses against 0.069 for strengths and 0.044 for whole reviews. The section that carries the decision is the section AI homogenizes hardest. The generation-side signature reappears here in miniature, inside a single proposal's criticism: human weakness panels are more even than chance (excess −0.006, −0.003) while pooled AI runs +0.026, and weaknesses are the only field where clumping is already significant at baseline (δ = +0.48).
AI panel scores do not predict funding outcomes. On the competition's 1-to-5 scoring scale, the human panel's scores vary more across reviewers of a proposal (standard deviation 0.74) than the AI panels' scores do in any condition (0.51 at baseline, 0.34 at one-at-a-time, 0.29 at persona). AI scores also vary less across proposals than human scores (between-proposal standard deviation 0.36, 0.54, and 0.52 against 0.62), leaving less signal with which to separate them. Against the competition's actual outcomes (14 funded, 9 not), AI panel scores carry no detectable funding signal: the area under the ROC curve is 0.50, 0.47, and 0.50 across conditions (label-permutation P = 0.99, 0.84, 0.99), compared with 0.77 for the human panel. This human comparison is only exploratory, because quantitative human scores exist for just 13 of 23 proposals and the funding ranking was itself set by human review, which gives the human panel a built-in advantage. The claim we stand behind is therefore the AI-side one: across all three conditions, AI panel scores show no detectable funding signal.

Result D: Neither style nor prompt explains the narrowing
Two alternatives would make the results so far artifacts rather than properties of the models: that differences between human and AI writing style manufacture the effect, and that better prompts dissolve it. Neither survives.
Style and embedding. Running every analysis on raw text as well as on style-normalized text separates effects that live in the ideas from effects that live in the writing. Three findings are robust to style. Geometric coverage narrowing holds on both branches and is stronger on raw text (0.60 to 0.89, significant in every condition). The evenness contrast is significant in all six branch-by-condition cells. And gate-keeping compression under one-at-a-time and persona holds at δ ≈ −0.9 to −1.0 on both branches. Three further effects are matters of style alone and vanish after normalization: on raw text every model's proposal cloud is significantly displaced, AI review panels appear to fall short of the human review span (δ ≈ −0.9, reversing to +0.37 to +0.44 after normalization), and the review clouds appear segregated.
Prompting. Persona conditioning is one of the strongest documented interventions against language-model homogeneity [CITE]. If the narrowing were an artifact of asking one model for many items at once, it should ease under one-at-a-time and dissolve under persona. It does neither. Under persona, AI proposal sets are no more varied in ideas than at baseline (richness 0.75 of human, P = 0.004; Fig. 2A), and the human-only fringe is at its widest (26%). What personas change is wording: the AI-to-human distinct-word ratio rises to about 0.95 under persona (0.93 to 0.96), up from 0.81 to 0.83 at one-at-a-time and 0.88 to 0.93 at baseline, nearly closing the wording gap while spread and richness stay put (Fig. 2C). Personas change how the models write, not what they propose. Because the primary branch normalizes style before embedding, this is a residual-wording result; on raw text, persona wording returns only to its baseline level.


DISCUSSION
We compared the outputs of a real grant competition with proposals and reviews generated by three frontier models from the same call and review criteria, measuring diversity across sets rather than the quality of any single item. Both stages compressed, but in different ways. In generation, AI sets contained substantially less variation in ideas and concentrated more heavily in the dense center of the human distribution. Human-defined distinctiveness strongly predicted whether AI would reproduce an idea: AI missed none of the densely represented core, 4% of the middle, and 69% of the distinctive edge. Across elicitation conditions, roughly one human proposal in five had no nearby AI counterpart, whereas only 4% to 9% of AI proposals lacked a nearby human counterpart. Yet the two groups engaged broadly similar biomedical topics and their proposal clouds were not detectably displaced. AI therefore does not search somewhere else; it reproduces the center of human scientific search while thinning its distinctive edges. Gate-keeping showed the complementary pattern. AI panels covered nearly all of the semantic span of human reviews but provided less independent judgment for nearly every proposal, with the strongest convergence in assessments of weaknesses. A pipeline that automated both stages would therefore narrow scientific search twice: first by reducing the range of distinctive possibilities considered, and then by evaluating those possibilities with a more correlated set of judgments.

The asymmetry matters more than the general conclusion that AI is less diverse because the two losses have different consequences. Generation is primarily a coverage problem. The models enter much the same scientific territory as humans but allocate more of their proposals to densely represented positions within it. Proposals missed more often by AI were not detectably less likely to be funded or lower ranked, suggesting that AI is not simply omitting ideas the human selection process itself discarded. Prompting also did little to recover this edge: generating proposals independently removed within-context dependence, and persona conditioning substantially altered wording, yet neither restored human-level semantic diversity. Review presents a different problem. Additional AI reviewers can recover more content, but repeated sampling from a common model does not necessarily recreate independent judgment. This distinction matters because the value of aggregation depends partly on contributors supplying nonredundant information [CITE]; when judgments are correlated, adding reviewers provides less of the benefit expected from independent contributors, as recognized in work on ensemble decision making [CITE], jury models [CITE], algorithmic monoculture [CITE], and deliberative disagreement [CITE]. Consistent with this logic, agreement between AI panel rankings and the competition ranking increased initially as reviewers were added but then leveled off to chance, whereas human-panel agreement continued to increase over the smaller range of panel sizes available. Preserving diversity at the two stages may therefore require different interventions: broader or more heterogeneous sources of ideas for generation, and genuinely independent sources of judgment for evaluation.

Several mechanisms may plausibly explain the compression. Training corpora of frontier AI models may concentrate around recurrent scientific questions, methods, and argumentative forms, giving models stronger priors over commonly represented combinations. Post-training fine-tuning may further favor outputs that appear broadly plausible or defensible, shifting probability toward modal solutions; a related tendency could help explain why convergence is particularly strong in criticism. Distinguishing these mechanisms would require access to training distributions, intermediate checkpoints, or controlled training- and decoding-level interventions.

These two forms of compression have particular consequences for efforts to automate science at scale. Much of the promise of AI-enabled science rests on scale: models can generate more hypotheses, design more experiments, read more papers, and evaluate more candidates than human scientists or committees could feasibly handle [CITE]. But scale expands scientific search only to the extent that additional candidates explore new parts of the possibility space and additional evaluations contribute independent information. Our results show how those assumptions can fail at both stages. If generation preferentially reproduces the dense core of existing ideas, producing a hundred or a thousand candidates need not expand the search proportionally; scale may instead increase sampling density around already well-represented possibilities while leaving distinctive directions comparatively underexplored. If selection is also automated, greater review volume need not provide a corresponding increase in independent judgment. We do not test an end-to-end automated pipeline and therefore cannot determine how these two forms of compression would interact. But their coexistence identifies a risk that evaluations of automated science based on throughput alone will miss: nominal scale and effective search are not the same thing. More hypotheses, experiments, or reviews expand scientific search only when they add sufficiently different possibilities or sufficiently independent judgments. This does not show that AI automation reduces innovation, which we do not measure directly. It suggests instead that as AI systems take on larger roles in scientific discovery and evaluation, the diversity preserved per unit of scale becomes an important property of the system itself.

The implications extend beyond grant review. Many decision systems generate candidates and then filter them: hiring, editorial screening, graduate admissions, venture evaluation, and regulatory review among them. Automation in these settings is usually evaluated one output or one decision at a time, yet neither the range of candidates considered nor the independence of the judgments applied to them is a property of any single output. An AI system could therefore improve the apparent quality of individual proposals or reviews while narrowing the collective search in which they operate. Our results make the distinction concrete: AI can remain within the same broad scientific domains as humans while undersampling the distinctive edges of their idea space, and AI reviewers can cover much of the semantic territory of human review while supplying less independent judgment. Evaluations of scientific AI should therefore measure set-level diversity and judgment independence alongside conventional measures of item-level quality and accuracy.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The study includes only 23 human proposals, limiting power for model-specific comparisons and making pooled contrasts the more informative tests; accordingly, we do not rank the models, and the prespecified ordering test did not support a consistent ordering. Some generation effects are sensitive to style normalization, although the core-edge relationship also appears in raw text. Distinctiveness and density are properties of this embedding representation and this particular proposal set, not universal classifications of the ideas themselves, and the core-middle-edge percentages should be interpreted as descriptive estimates rather than population constants. Review effects are highly consistent in direction but small in absolute semantic-distance units, and the strengths-versus-weaknesses analysis decomposes the same proposal comparisons rather than providing an independent replication. The decision-outcome analysis is particularly limited: human quantitative scores were available for only 14 proposals, confidence intervals are wide, and the absence of a detectable funding signal in AI scores is not evidence of a true null or of superior human discrimination. Likewise, the lack of an association between AI miss-rate and funding does not establish that distinctive proposals are more valuable; it shows only that they were not detectably less successful in this competition. Finally, the study concerns one competition, one proposal format, one scientific domain, three models, and one period in a rapidly changing technology. The next questions are whether core-edge compression persists across models and domains, whether heterogeneous model ensembles or deliberately atypical conditioning can recover the missing edge, and whether reduced reviewer independence ultimately changes allocation decisions.

Materials and Methods

**Corpus.** The human corpus is the complete set of 23 proposals submitted to the inaugural competition of the NSF National Synthesis Center for Emergence in the Molecular and Cellular Sciences (NCEMS), drawn from two applicant cohorts, together with the 85 expert reviews they received (2 to 5 reviewers per proposal, mean 3.70) and the program's own outcomes for each proposal: a binary funding decision (14 funded, 9 not) and a numeric ranking. Reviewers also recorded an overall score on the program's 1-to-5 scale, available for 13 to 14 of the 23 proposals. The proposals, reviews, and outcomes are confidential to the program and were never publicly released, so they are absent from any model's training corpus; this is the property that lets us treat the AI outputs as generated rather than recalled. Use of the de-identified corpus was covered under [agreement/approval; SI Appendix].

**AI generation and review.** Three frontier large language models, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini, and OpenAI's GPT (exact model versions, decoding parameters, and full prompts in SI Appendix), each drafted 23 proposals from the same call given to human applicants and reviewed the same 23 human proposals under the same criteria and scoring rubric given to human reviewers, producing 5 reviews per model per proposal (345 AI reviews in all). Holding the call, the proposals under review, and the criteria fixed across humans and models leaves the source of the text as the only varying factor.

**Elicitation conditions.** Each model generated and reviewed under three conditions of increasing pressure toward diversity. In *baseline*, a single request returns the whole set of 23 items. In *one-at-a-time*, each item comes from an independent call, so the model cannot condition a new item on the ones already produced. In *persona*, each independent call is additionally conditioned on a distinct scientist persona matched to a real applicant. Persona conditioning is among the strongest reported interventions against language-model homogeneity [CITE], so it functions as a stress test: narrowing that survives persona is unlikely to be a prompting artifact.

**Text normalization.** Because differences in writing style can make two texts appear dissimilar when their content is the same, every proposal and review, human and AI, was rewritten into a single neutral format and style by Gemini before embedding; this style-normalized text (the *rephrased* branch) is primary throughout. The unedited text (the *original* branch) was analyzed in parallel and is reported as a robustness check (SI Appendix). The gap between the two branches is itself a measurement: an effect present on both lives in the ideas, whereas an effect present only on raw text lives in the prose.

**Embedding and distance.** Each document was embedded with BioLinkBERT-large, a biomedical language model, using mean pooling followed by L2 normalization, yielding one 1,024-dimensional vector per document; dissimilarity is cosine distance throughout. For the field analysis of reviews, the strengths and weakness sections were embedded separately (rephrased branch only).

**Diversity facets.** We decomposed set diversity into four facets, each posing a different question of a set of proposals (or of a review panel). *Spread* is the mean pairwise cosine distance within a set. *Richness* is the Vendi score at order 1, the effective number of distinct items implied by the set's similarity structure. *Evenness* is the excess close-neighbor mass relative to a same-size random draw from the pooled items, positive values indicating near-duplicate clumping and negative values indicating more regular spacing than chance. *Coverage* is measured two ways: geometrically, as the fraction of human items with an AI item in their local neighborhood, benchmarked against how well one random half of the human set covers the other; and by literature region (below). To confirm that each facet reflects the data rather than one formula, every facet was additionally computed with several alternative estimators (for example, spread six ways, coverage two ways), which agree (SI Appendix).

**Literature grounding.** To test whether AI engages the same areas of biomedical science, we assembled a reference corpus of approximately 39,500 PubMed abstracts retrieved with search terms taken from the call, embedded them in the same space, and clustered them into 12 topic regions with BERTopic (the outlier bin was dropped). Each proposal was assigned to its nearest region and to its 10 nearest abstracts, from which we counted the literature regions engaged and the unique MeSH subject headings touched. Treating the regions as categories, we report the categorical inverse-Simpson effective number of regions occupied, which captures how evenly a set spreads across the literature rather than how many regions it reaches.

**Core versus edge.** To ask whether the human proposals AI fails to reproduce are specifically the distinctive ones, we measured, for each of the 23 human proposals, its peripherality in human idea space using human-to-human distances only (distance from the human centroid, mean distance to its three nearest human neighbors, and local density within the human 90th-percentile nearest-neighbor distance) and, separately, its miss-rate: the fraction of equal-sized (n = 23) AI pools, across conditions and subsamples, whose nearest proposal to that human idea falls beyond the same 90th-percentile yardstick. Peripherality is defined from human geometry alone and miss-rate from human-to-AI distances, so the two share no inputs. We summarized their relationship with Spearman correlation and, as a quality guard, tested whether miss-rate was associated with the program's funding decision or ranking (Mann-Whitney and Spearman; SI Appendix).

**Gate-keeping design.** Because reviews of different proposals differ for reasons unrelated to reviewer independence, every review facet is computed within a proposal: the 2 to 5 human reviews of a proposal against equal-sized panels drawn from that proposal's AI reviews, matched to the human panel size for that proposal. The spread, richness, evenness, and coverage measures are as above, with two review-specific benchmarks stated on every figure: panel coverage is judged against leave-one-out self-coverage (how well each human review is covered by the rest of its own panel, 0.83 on average), and panel clumping against random same-size draws from that condition's review pool. The full battery was run three times over a field axis, the whole review, its strengths section alone, and its weakness section alone (rephrased branch only), to locate where the compression concentrates.

**Score and decision analyses.** Using the reviewers' numeric overall scores and the program's real outcomes, we compared human and AI panels on within-panel score spread, on rank agreement with the funding ranking as a function of panel size, and on the separation of funded from not-funded proposals by panel mean score (area under the ROC curve). Each AUC carries a stratified bootstrap 95% confidence interval and a two-sided label-permutation p-value (10,000 draws each). Because human scores cover only part of the set and the funding ranking was itself produced by human review, the human-versus-AI contrast is exploratory and is reported as such; a size- and proposal-matched decomposition, which leaves the AI-side conclusion unchanged, is given in SI Appendix.

**Wording controls.** To confirm that the semantic results are not an artifact of the embedding model, we measured two text-only quantities that never touch the embedding, distinct-2gram (the share of unique adjacent word pairs) and self-BLEU (self-repetition), for humans and for each model on both text branches.

**Statistical inference.** Every comparison is made at matched sample size. Generation contrasts compare whole sets at 23 versus 23; pooled AI is the three models' 69 proposals subsampled to 23 without replacement, averaged over 1,000 draws, and reported as an AI-to-human ratio in which 1.0 is human parity. Group differences use label-permutation tests (10,000 shuffles) and confidence intervals use the jackknife; we never resample with replacement, which would inject duplicates into metrics that are sensitive to them. Gate-keeping is a paired design and is reported as Cliff's δ, the consistency with which the AI panel falls below the human panel across the 23 proposals, with p-values from the exact paired Wilcoxon test. Five comparisons were designated primary in advance, one headline metric for each of the four facets (spread, richness, evenness, and geometric coverage) plus a whole-cloud displacement check, and are read at face value; all other p-values are corrected for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure applied within each task, text branch, and field stratum. Displacement between two clouds was tested with the kernel two-sample statistic MMD² against a shuffle null and cross-checked with an optimal-transport (Wasserstein) distance.
