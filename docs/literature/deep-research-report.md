# Embedding-Based Semantic Distance for Novelty and Diversity Assessment in Research and Ideas

## What the literature actually measures

Across the primary literature, **novelty** and **diversity** are not treated as a single thing. In scientific-research studies, novelty is usually operationalized in one of three ways: **recombinant novelty** (a work combines prior knowledge elements that are semantically far apart), **element novelty** (a work introduces content that is distant from the prior document universe), or **local-density novelty** (a work sits in a sparse neighborhood of semantic space). In creativity and ideation studies, novelty is usually closer to **originality** or **semantic remoteness** from common ideas, while diversity is usually a **set-level property**—how varied a pool of ideas is, or how many distinct semantic regions it spans. This distinction matters for proposal evaluation: a proposal can be highly novel but not diverse relative to a proposal pool, and a portfolio can be highly diverse even if each proposal is only moderately novel. citeturn47view3turn10view0turn14view0turn8view3turn39view4turn30view0

The scientific-literature papers I found are concentrated around corpora such as entity["organization","PubMed","biomedical literature database"] and entity["organization","Web of Science","citation index"], whereas the creativity literature mostly uses divergent-thinking tasks, word-association tasks, short narratives, or classroom ideation. An important implication is that **direct ex ante evaluation of full scientific research proposals remains relatively rare** in the embedding literature; most evidence comes from published papers, generated ideas, and creativity tasks, plus a smaller recent literature on LLM-assisted novelty review of research ideas and papers. That means a proposal-evaluation framework should borrow validated pieces from adjacent literatures rather than claim that one existing metric has already solved the proposal problem. This is an inference from the surveyed corpus, not a direct claim from any single paper. citeturn47view3turn10view0turn14view0turn42view0turn38view0turn33view1turn18view0turn46search0

## Embedding-based methods across scientific research, ideation, and writing

In published-science evaluation, one influential line is the recombinant-novelty approach of **Shibayama et al. (2021)**. They use **scispaCy** word embeddings in a **200-dimensional** space, build a vector for each **cited reference** by averaging the embeddings of words in its title, abstract, or keywords, compute **cosine distance** between every pair of cited references, and then aggregate those pairwise distances with a **q-percentile statistic** (`Novel_q`). Higher-performing variants use high percentiles, especially `q = 100`, which effectively asks whether the paper contains at least one especially distant recombination among its cited inputs. This is a particularly relevant template for proposal review when you want to ask whether a proposal combines prior literatures in an unusual way. citeturn9view0turn47view1turn47view3

A complementary line is **element novelty**, developed by **Yin et al. (2023)**. Instead of focusing on relationships among references, they trained a **300-dimensional** word-embedding model on scientific text across fields, assigned each paper a document vector by taking the **mean of title-and-abstract word vectors**, computed **cosine distance** from that paper to all prior papers, and then used **percentiles of the distance distribution** as novelty scores. Their low-percentile statistics are intentionally important: if even the nearest prior papers are still far away, the paper is semantically unusual in a stronger sense than simple average distance would suggest. This is closer to the question “is this proposal unlike what already exists?” rather than “does it recombine distant inputs?” citeturn10view0turn9view5

A more domain-specific biomedical version appears in **Liu et al. (2022)**, who represent scientific novelty as unusual **bio-entity combinations**. They extract entities from titles and abstracts with **PubTator**, embed the entities with **BioBERT**, compute **cosine distance** between entity pairs, define the **upper 10th percentile** of pairwise distances as “novel” combinations, and score each paper by the **proportion of novel entity pairs** among all possible entity pairs in that paper. Technically, this is still a semantic-distance method, but it operates on extracted scientific entities rather than on full-document embeddings. That makes it appealing when proposal reviewers care about whether a proposal links biologically distant mechanisms, molecules, or phenomena. citeturn14view0turn13view3

Recent biomedical work pushes farther into large-model embeddings. **Peng et al. (2025)** introduce **SemNovel**, which uses **LLM-based text embeddings** to place entity["organization","PubMed","biomedical literature database"] articles into a shared semantic universe and computes novelty from their distances to prior literature. The paper’s abstract reports that the method uses **BAAI/llm-embedder**, projects the corpus into a 2D semantic map for visualization, and that the public code computes an **average distance to k nearest prior neighbors** (`auc_dk`, default `k = 100`). In other words, SemNovel belongs to the “distance to prior corpus” family, but uses a much richer contextual encoder than earlier count-based or static-embedding systems. citeturn36search3turn16view0

A closely related but methodologically distinct recent proposal is the **Relative Neighbor Density** approach of **Wang et al. (2025)**. Their pipeline embeds ideas and literature with an **M3-Embedding / BGE-M3** model, finds **P nearest neighbors** of a candidate idea, computes each item’s **neighbor density** as the mean **cosine distance** to its **Q nearest neighbors**, and scores novelty as the **empirical rank** of the idea’s own neighbor density relative to those of its neighbors, scaled to **0–100**. This is more robust than raw nearest-neighbor distance when different fields have different baseline densities, and it is explicitly designed for cross-domain research-idea evaluation rather than only within a single literature. citeturn7view4turn8view3

In the creativity and ideation literature, **Beaty and Johnson’s SemDis** is a landmark. Across five studies, they compare **five semantic models**—including **GloVe**, **continuous bag-of-words** variants, and **TASA/LSA**—and show that a **latent factor across multiple semantic models** predicts human originality and novelty ratings better than reliance on any single space alone. For multiword responses they recommend a **multiplicative compositional model**, which substantially improved correspondence with human judgments. Technically, SemDis is usually a **prompt-to-response semantic distance** measure for short creative responses, making it the most established automated originality metric for short-form ideation. citeturn42view0turn43view2turn43view3

For longer-form writing, **Johnson et al. (2023)** introduce **Divergent Semantic Integration**, or DSI. They compare **six distributional-semantic models**, find that **BERT** performs best, and define DSI as the **mean pairwise cosine distance among all words in a narrative**. The conceptual move is important: instead of measuring remoteness from a prompt, DSI measures how strongly the text itself integrates semantically divergent ideas. That is much closer to what many people informally mean by “idea diversity inside a proposal.” citeturn38view0turn39view1

In educational ideation, **Ul Haq et al. (2024)** compare **eight sentence-embedding models** on open-ended cocreative ideas from small groups of students. Their pipeline first embeds each idea as a sentence vector, then computes **cosine similarity** between idea vectors, and treats those similarities as the basis for novelty assessment. They explicitly compare models including **USE-T**, **USE-DAN**, **all-MiniLM-L6**, and **all-mpnet-base**, and the best-performing models are the ones whose scores best match expert consensus. This is highly relevant to proposal evaluation because proposals are usually sentence- and paragraph-level objects, not isolated keywords. citeturn33view1turn33view2turn33view3

For **diversity** specifically, the strongest technical pattern is that set-level diversity benefits from **multiple statistics**, not just one. In **Interpretable Directed Diversity**, ideations are represented with the **Universal Sentence Encoder**, and diversity is computed with three complementary metrics: **Ideation Dispersion** (minimum-spanning-tree mean edge weight), **Ideation Disparity** (mean pairwise distance), and **Repeller Chamfer Distance** (mean minimum pairwise distance). The paper explicitly notes that these capture **different aspects of diversity** and can move differently under intervention. That is directly applicable to proposal panels or portfolios, where “broadly spread” and “far from nearest neighbor” are not the same thing. citeturn29view0turn30view0turn30view1turn31view0

## How well these measures are validated against ground truth

The validation evidence is strongest in **short-form creativity and ideation tasks**. In **SemDis**, the multiplicative latent semantic-distance factor explains **83% of the variance** in human creativity ratings in one AUT analysis, and in word-association tasks the correlations with human **novelty** ratings become extremely high—approaching unity in one sample. The same paper also shows convergent validity with creative metaphor production, creative self-efficacy, and some creativity-related behavioral measures. That is strong evidence that semantic distance can capture a large share of what human raters mean by originality in constrained verbal tasks. citeturn43view2turn44view2turn42view0

The writing literature shows similarly strong but narrower evidence. In **DSI**, the best **BERT-based** model explains **up to 72% of the variance** in human creativity ratings for narratives and performs similarly for expert and nonexpert raters across more than 3,500 short narratives. This is one of the best pieces of evidence that contextual embeddings can recover a substantial share of human judgments in longer-form text—not only in one prompt, but across multiple prompts and participant groups. citeturn38view0

The classroom ideation study by **Ul Haq et al. (2024)** also validates well against human judgment, though on a smaller and more domain-specific dataset. The authors report that **USE-T** correlates most strongly with agreed expert scores (`r = 0.860`, Spearman `= 0.784`), followed by **USE-DAN**, **all-MiniLM-L6**, and **all-mpnet-base**, and they also examine **R²**, **RMSE**, and class-probability alignment across low/medium/high novelty categories. This is a good example of validation that goes beyond a single correlation coefficient. citeturn33view0turn33view3turn34view3

The evidence is more mixed—but still meaningful—for **published scientific papers**. **Shibayama et al. (2021)** validate their recombinant novelty metric three ways: by showing it relates to an established **co-citation distance** between documents, by correlating it with **self-reported novelty/newness**, and by showing it predicts whether a paper reaches the **top 1% of citation impact** later on. **Yin et al. (2023)** similarly validate their element-novelty metric against an established embedding model, against a document-distance benchmark, and against **self-reported novelty** from a survey of roughly **800 scientists**. These are serious validation efforts, but they are still imperfect for proposal review because future citations and author self-reports are only partial proxies for true novelty. citeturn9view0turn47view1turn47view3turn9view5turn9view6turn10view0

More recent science-of-science approaches often validate against **indirect downstream indicators** rather than expert judgments. **SemNovel** reports that its scores correlate with later **citation counts** better than prior content-based novelty indicators and that it identifies highly novel papers such as Nobel-winning work. **RND** reports strong **AUROC** values on computer-science, biomedical, and mixed-domain benchmarks, but its “ground truth” is constructed from **recent top-tier papers** as positives versus **older highly cited papers** as negatives. That is a clever scalable benchmark, but it also bakes in assumptions about recency, prestige, and what “novel” should look like. For proposal evaluation, those assumptions are useful as external validity checks, not as gold truth. citeturn15search0turn36search3turn7view4turn8view2turn7view5

Taken together, the validation record supports a careful claim: **semantic-distance measures are credible proxies for one important component of novelty and diversity**, especially semantic remoteness and coverage of distinct conceptual regions. But they are **not fully validated stand-ins for holistic scientific merit**, and the evidence is much stronger for **short creative tasks** than for **ex ante scientific proposals**. That conclusion follows from the asymmetry of the validation literature described above. citeturn42view0turn38view0turn33view3turn47view3turn7view5

## How these methods compare with expert review, bibliometrics, and qualitative coding

Compared with expert review, embedding metrics are **cheap, scalable, reproducible, and temporally explicit**. Once the corpus cutoff, embedding model, and distance function are frozen, the same proposal gets the same score every time. They are also often better than purely lexical methods at recognizing paraphrase and conceptual relatedness. But papers in both the creativity and idea-review literatures repeatedly note their weaknesses: scores depend on the **training corpus**, the **semantic model**, and the **aggregation rule**; they can be biased by **text length** or by missing domain vocabulary; and they usually do not explain themselves in a way a reviewer or applicant can readily audit. citeturn42view0turn43view3turn38view0turn7view5

Expert review remains the richest ex ante method because experts can judge not only semantic newness but also **plausibility, scientific value, risk, and what the field already knows but has not written down clearly**. At the same time, recent proposal/idea-review papers are blunt that novelty judgments are subjective and difficult to articulate. The **Idea Novelty Checker** paper states that experts often find it easier to judge similarity than to explain why one idea is more novel than another, and its whole system is built to ground judgments in retrieved literature and facet-by-facet comparison. citeturn18view0

The strongest current proposal-style review template I found is in *Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas?*. Their expert review form asks reviewers to score novelty separately from other criteria, using the question: **“Whether the idea is creative and different from existing works on the topic, and brings fresh insights.”** Reviewers are explicitly encouraged to search for related work, and the same form separately records **familiarity**, **feasibility**, **expected effectiveness**, and a free-text rationale. That separation is empirically justified in the paper because overall scores correlate strongly with **novelty** and **excitement**, but barely with **feasibility**, meaning raters otherwise risk collapsing distinct dimensions into one overall impression. citeturn21view1turn21view2turn19view0

Bibliometric approaches remain important comparators, especially unusual-reference-combination measures and citation-based downstream measures. They are excellent for **large historical analyses**, but they are weaker for **proposal evaluation**, because proposals do not yet have citations and because citation outcomes mix novelty with visibility, field size, prestige, and diffusion dynamics. Even in the published-paper literature, the construct validity of bibliometric novelty indicators remains debated; the critique by **Bornmann et al. (2019)** explicitly investigates whether unusual-combination indicators measure what they claim to measure. citeturn17search8turn47view3

Qualitative coding sits between expert review and metrics. It is slower and costlier than embeddings, but much more interpretable: a coder can label whether a proposal is novel in its **problem framing**, **method**, **data**, **theory**, or **finding** contribution. The semantic-network work by **Hou et al. (2022)** is interesting here because it tries to recover originality through relationships among “knowledge units” and their semantic similarity rather than only through citations or distances to the full corpus. Still, compared with facet coding by trained humans, these methods remain less transparent about *why* a proposal is being judged as novel. citeturn36search1turn18view0

## A robust framework for evaluating novelty and diversity in scientific research proposals

The most defensible design for **scientific research proposals** is a **multi-method framework**, not a single score. The semantic layer should use **at least two novelty metrics** and **at least two diversity metrics**. For novelty, I would combine one **local-density metric** and one **distance-to-prior-corpus metric**. Concretely, that means something like: nearest-neighbor or neighbor-density novelty in the spirit of **RND**; plus a corpus-distance percentile in the spirit of **Yin et al.** or **SemNovel**; and, if the proposal includes references, a recombinant metric in the spirit of **Shibayama et al.** Diversity should be computed at the **set level**, not the single-proposal level, using measures analogous to **mean pairwise distance**, **MST dispersion**, and **mean minimum distance**, because the ideation literature shows these metrics capture different aspects of diversity. citeturn8view3turn10view0turn36search3turn47view3turn30view0turn30view1

The expert-review layer should be **blind, calibrated, and explicitly multidimensional**. The literature supports separating novelty from feasibility and impact, collecting reviewer familiarity, and requiring a rationale that cites similar prior work when the novelty score is low. The closest directly reusable wording I found is the novelty prompt from the idea-review form above, combined with the structure of creativity-rating studies that give short anchored scales rather than vague open-ended judgments. If you need exact precedent examples from the literature, the closest match is: DSI raters were asked to **“evaluate each story on a scale of 1 (very uncreative) to 5 (very creative)”**; SemDis AUT raters used **“1 (not at all creative) to 5 (very creative)”**; and the classroom study had experts assign similarity scores from **0 (completely dissimilar) to 1 (completely similar)** before reaching consensus. Those exact precedents are useful because they show that successful automated systems are usually trained or validated against **simple, anchored human judgments**, not against vague reviewer prose. citeturn39view1turn44view1turn34view1

The LLM-review layer should be **retrieval-grounded and facet-decomposed**. The most helpful paper here is **Idea Novelty Checker**, which uses broad retrieval, **embedding-based filtering**, and then an LLM reranker that compares candidate ideas to prior literature along **purpose, mechanism, evaluation, and application** facets. It also finds that **expert-annotated in-context examples** materially improve performance, and warns that prompt wording matters enough to change outcomes. For proposal review, the practical implication is simple: an LLM should never judge novelty from the proposal text alone. It should be shown retrieved prior work, asked to compare by explicit facets, and asked to return a **score plus literature-grounded rationale plus uncertainty statement**. citeturn18view0

For rigor and reproducibility, I would recommend the following protocol. First, create a **small gold set** of proposals or idea summaries scored independently by experts on novelty and diversity. Second, fit and compare several embedding pipelines on that gold set rather than assuming one encoder is best; the literature repeatedly shows that model choice matters. Third, freeze the **corpus cutoff date**, **embedding model version**, **distance metric**, and **aggregation rule** before full deployment. Fourth, report both **criterion validity** (correlation with expert scores) and **external validity** (where possible, later downstream outcomes), but do not treat downstream outcomes as the sole truth. Fifth, audit for **length effects**, **domain-coverage failures**, and **reviewer-familiarity effects**. Sixth, use LLMs only as a **third reviewer-like signal**, never as a replacement for experts or for the semantic metrics themselves. citeturn42view0turn38view0turn33view3turn7view5turn21view1

If the goal is later prompt design for LLMs, the literature supports a prompt that asks about **new problem framing**, **new elements**, **unusual combinations**, and **distance from existing work**, but it also suggests two important safeguards. First, keep novelty **separate** from feasibility and expected effectiveness, because reviewers otherwise conflate them. Second, require the model to cite or summarize the **most similar prior work** that motivated the score. That makes the LLM review more like the best human-review forms and much easier to validate. In short: use embeddings to measure semantic position, experts to judge scientific worth, and LLMs to produce structured, literature-grounded comparison and rationale. That combination is the most robust and research-grounded design I found for scientific research proposals. citeturn19view0turn21view1turn18view0turn46search0
