## Introduction

Scientific funding shapes the range of questions researchers can pursue. Investigators formulate candidate explanations and approaches, and reviewers assess their originality, significance, and feasibility. Together, these activities determine which scientific alternatives receive consideration and resources. Large language models (LLMs) could expand the capacity to generate proposals and provide feedback, but greater output does not necessarily enlarge the range of scientific possibilities considered. Evaluating their role in funding therefore requires attention to both the composition of proposed research and the distinctions made during its assessment.

Recent advances make this question increasingly consequential. LLM-generated research ideas have received favorable expert assessments of novelty, even as their generators exhibit limited diversity across repeated outputs ([Si et al., 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/ea94957d81b1c1caf87ef5319fa6b467-Abstract-Conference.html)). Scientific-ideation systems now retrieve literature, recombine research components, and revise suggestions against prior work ([Wang et al., 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.18/); [Radensky et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14634)). Yet sophisticated generation does not guarantee more compelling ideas: a large interdisciplinary evaluation found that knowledge-graph-selected concept pairs did not improve expert-rated interest over a titles-only LLM baseline ([Gu and Krenn, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2611349123)). These findings distinguish the quality of individual suggestions from the breadth of a generated collection and make generation strategy an empirical part of the question.

Evidence of collective concentration already spans several settings. AI assistance can improve individual creative outputs while making them more similar across writers ([Doshi and Hauser, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)), and AI-augmented scientific research has been associated with individual advantages alongside a narrower collective focus ([Hao et al., 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09922-y)). Most directly, Qian and colleagues find that greater detected LLM involvement in grant submissions is associated with lower semantic distinctiveness relative to previously funded research ([Qian et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15485v3)). A recent preprint also reports concentrated scientific exploration across multiple research-agent frameworks and distinguishes changes in research questions from changes in methods ([Tang and Yang, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27905v2)). Together, this work motivates a more specific question: which scientific alternatives are underrepresented, and what is their significance within an actual funding competition?

Answering that question requires distinguishing scientific content from its expression. LLM rewriting can homogenize linguistic features while retaining core content ([Sourati et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11266v2)). Conversely, proposals with similar vocabulary can advance different explanations or use the same method to resolve different uncertainties. Research on scientific novelty emphasizes unusual combinations of prior knowledge and distinguishes novelty in questions, methods, and their combinations ([Uzzi et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1240474); [Luo et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2022.101282)). These distinctions imply several forms of concentration: repeated use of familiar approaches, omission of uncommon questions, or underrepresentation of particular question–method connections despite coverage of their individual components. A lower aggregate diversity score cannot distinguish these possibilities. Nor does rarity alone establish scientific value: an unusual proposal may introduce a productive departure, an unsupported conjecture, or a mismatch with the call.

Detailed expert reviews provide a way to investigate what these differences mean scientifically. Reviews identify the contributions experts recognize, the evidence they find persuasive, and the reasons they regard an approach as promising, risky, or flawed. Prior research shows that LLM feedback can be useful to authors ([Liang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196)), while comparisons of review panels document excessive agreement among AI reviewers ([Baumann et al., 2026](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/baumann26a.html)). Linking reviews to particular proposal contributions allows a further distinction between recognizing an idea and endorsing it. A model might rarely generate an unusual hypothesis yet assess it effectively when presented; alternatively, it might overlook the same scientific departure during both generation and evaluation.

We therefore ask three connected questions. Do model-generated collections cover the scientific questions, explanations, and approaches represented in submissions to the same call, beyond the omissions expected from finite human samples? Which components and combinations account for any coverage deficit, and how do expert reviews characterize their originality, promise, and limitations? Finally, can literature grounding and deliberate recombination recover these alternatives while preserving scientific coherence and feasibility? These questions treat diversity as the structure of the alternatives available for consideration, with expert appraisal supplying evidence about their scientific meaning.

Our starting point is a molecular and cellular bioscience competition at the National Synthesis Center for Emergence in the Molecular and Cellular Sciences (NCEMS), comprising 23 proposals and 85 expert reviews. Exploratory comparisons with proposals generated under the same call suggest that AI represents densely occupied regions of the submitted proposal space more readily than its distinctive edges. This pattern motivates the expanded investigation; its interpretation as selective omission of scientific ideas requires further content validation and a human-sampling benchmark. A newly available archive from another major funding agency provides approximately 14,000 proposals across 600 calls from 2019 to 2026, including all submissions, their reviews, original calls and evaluation criteria, and recorded funding outcomes. This setting will allow us to examine how the pattern varies across research areas, funding priorities, and time.

The expanded analysis will connect proposal geometry to specific scientific content. Building on scientific-claim extraction and component-based representations, we will identify questions, hypotheses where applicable, approaches, and intended contributions, retaining their relationships and supporting passages ([Magnusson and Friedman, 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.381/); [Radensky et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14634)). Expert validation will test whether apparent matches preserve the substantive contribution and whether apparent omissions survive changes in wording. Comparisons within calls will assess component and combination coverage against equally sized human reference sets, while historical literature measures will distinguish local rarity from unusual connections to prior knowledge. Generation experiments will compare the existing independent-request approach with literature-grounded and structured-recombination conditions, separating the information supplied from the procedure used to explore it.

For the same submitted proposals, we will examine how human and AI reviews recognize and appraise distinctive contributions, and how much additional valid, proposal-specific information successive reviews provide. Recorded funding outcomes will connect these assessments to institutional choices without treating acceptance as definitive evidence of scientific merit. Analyses of LLM-associated writing and linguistic diversity over time will characterize the changing submission population, while content validation will address whether the measured differences concern expression or scientific ideas. We use double compression as a hypothesis about selective representation at two stages: which alternatives generation makes available and which distinctions evaluation preserves. The resulting design asks when AI expands scientific choice, when it concentrates that choice, and which changes in its use can recover underrepresented possibilities.

## Editorial notes

- The final three paragraphs describe the planned extension. Replace prospective wording and add a concise findings preview only after those analyses are completed. Do not insert an assumed replication or a predicted intervention failure as a result.
- The NCEMS sample sizes follow the current manuscript. The new archive's counts and coverage follow the researcher's description and require confirmation against the received data, including the status of 2026 decisions.
- The argument distinguishes scientific ideas from wording without suggesting that Qian or the broader ideation literature studies only style. Tang and Yang is explicitly identified as a preprint.
- The introduction motivates expert validation and stronger generation conditions without claiming that persona prompting exhausts plausible alternatives. Exact prompts, matching budgets, extraction procedures, and dated retrieval belong in Methods.
- Confidentiality is not presented as proof that models have never encountered proposals or related work. Historical current-model comparisons and public-exposure checks require treatment in Methods and limitations.
- Double compression denotes two linked questions, not a demonstrated compounding effect in an automated selection pipeline. The current design does not include historical human reviews of newly generated AI proposals.
- This draft uses scientific-content extraction as a prospective measurement strategy. No new content labels, prevalence estimates, or agency results have been computed for this introduction.
